Agents, and why Hermes

What an agent is, and why we start people on Hermes.

What's an agent?

You already use AI models through products like Claude or ChatGPT: you ask a question, and the model answers.

An agent goes a step further. It connects a model to instructions, tools, and usually some form of memory. Instead of only producing an answer, it can take actions and work through a series of steps—such as searching the web, editing files, sending a message, or running a scheduled task.

Agents are not automatically independent or trustworthy. You decide what the agent can access, what it is allowed to do, and when it needs your approval. Today, you'll build an agent that you control.

The words you'll hear:
  • Model — the brain you talk to (Claude, GPT, Gemini)
  • Agent — a model plus memory, scheduling, tools, and a personality
  • Framework — what you can build a agent with. Hermes is one; OpenClaw is another
  • Virtual machine — a computer you rent in the cloud, separate from your laptop

What's Hermes?

Hermes is the agent framework we'll use in this course. It connects an AI model to persistent memory, tools, reusable skills, messaging platforms, and scheduled tasks.

Another popular option is OpenClaw. Both are powerful, self-hosted systems, and both require thoughtful security settings. We're beginning with Hermes because its structure, built-in tools, and safety controls make it a good fit for the way this course demos agent building.

That does not mean the agent is automatically safe. During setup, we'll decide who is allowed to talk to it, which tools it can use, which actions require approval, and what information it should be able to access.

Why build your own?

When you use a hosted assistant, its memory, settings, and behavior live inside that company's product. A self-hosted agent gives you more control over how those pieces are stored and configured.

With Hermes, much of your agent's identity lives in files and data on a machine you control: its instructions, memories, skills, schedules, and configuration. Because the framework supports multiple AI providers, you can change the model it uses without rebuilding the entire agent.

You might begin with OpenAI Codex, move to a Claude model later, or configure a backup model for times when your primary provider is unavailable or rate-limited. This is especially helpful when one provider e.g. GPT Sol is suddenly competitive (and better in some cases) than Claude.

You can also give the agent its own identity in shared communication spaces. For example, it can participate in a Discord, Slack, Telegram, Matrix, or WhatsApp channel and respond according to rules you define.

That is one thing we will set up today: not just an AI you chat with, but an agent that can remember its role, use approved tools, and work with you and other people.

When you don't need an agent

A lot of the time, you do not need to build your own agent.

If you want to summarize this week's email, prepare for your next meeting, analyze a document, or complete another well-defined task, start with a hosted assistant like Claude or ChatGPT. These products already connect to many of the tools people use every day, and they are becoming more agentic all the time.

Use the simplest tool that can reliably do the job.

Hermes (or something like it) begins to make sense when you want an agent that:

  • keeps working across many conversations and tasks;
  • remembers information outside a single company's app;
  • runs recurring or scheduled workflows;
  • uses several tools as part of the same job;
  • can be reached by you and other trusted people;
  • has an identity, role, and set of rules that persist over time; or
  • can change model providers without being rebuilt from scratch.

The point of this course is not that everyone needs a self-hosted agent.

We are building one because it is a useful way to understand the fundamentals: how models use tools, how memory works, how permissions are set, how scheduled work runs, and where the risks come from. Once you understand those pieces, the agentic features appearing inside Claude, ChatGPT, Codex, and whatever comes next become much less mysterious.

There is also a practical reason. A self-hosted framework gives you more control over your agent's instructions, memories, skills, and data. You can choose which model providers it uses instead of designing the entire system around one company's product.

Why a blank computer

An agent that can write and run its own code, sitting on your laptop, can reach everything else on your laptop: your files, your tax returns, whatever is there.

An agent can misunderstand what you asked, make an unexpected change, or follow a malicious instruction hidden in something it reads. You might have heard the story of someone's whole inbox getting wiped. That's why we're starting cautiously.

In Part 1 you put your agent on a rented, blank machine with none of your data on it. You hand over only what you choose.
